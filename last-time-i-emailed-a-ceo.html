The Last Time I Emailed a CEO, They Shipped a Feature

Around 2019, I sent an email straight to Amazon's CEO about a small, maddening thing: from a listing, you couldn't tell whether an item was even returnable. My issue got resolved — and not long after, amazon.in listings started carrying "10 days returnable" and "10 days replacement only" labels. I'll never be able to prove my email caused it. But I like to think it nudged the right software teams into shipping something.

So when Amazon handed me a fresh piece of absurdity this month, I knew exactly where to send it.

The setup

I ordered something worth ₹3,283. What arrived wasn't what the listing showed. I filed a return — wrong item sent — and did the diligent thing: I photographed the wrong items and uploaded the pictures along with my request.

I keep a dropbox outside my front door — a homemade version of the return bins Amazon installs inside Whole Foods. Packages land there; returns can be collected from there. I placed the return item inside, set it aside, ready to go.

Where it goes sideways

On February 8th, the pickup agent called. I told him exactly where the item was: in the dropbox by the door. I came home later to find he'd taken a different package — one from another seller entirely — and left my actual return sitting untouched.

Here's the part that should worry Amazon more than it worries me: my refund came through anyway. The ₹3,283 was credited. Which means the wrong package was collected, carried to a warehouse, and checked in against my return — with no one noticing it bore no resemblance to the photos I had uploaded.

Sit with that chain for a second. I photographed the item. Amazon has the photos. The one person who needed them — the agent standing at my door — was never shown them. And the warehouse that processed the return matched nothing to anything.

The bill for that gap

I'm now out a ₹1,190 package that belonged to me, gone because a delivery agent picked up the wrong box — knowingly or not. Call it what it plainly is: my goods left my possession without my consent.

So who do I hold responsible?

  • The agent who grabbed the wrong package?
  • The warehouse that processes returns without checking items against the photos on file?
  • The customer support line that offered me no resolution at all?

On that support call, I laid out three clean ways to make it right:

  1. Refund me for the invoice value of the package I lost.
  2. Retrieve my package from wherever it now sits.
  3. Email the seller, explain the mix-up, and direct them to return it to me.

None of the three happened. Which is worth pausing on, because "customer support" is supposed to contain the word support.

Which leaves one question for the people at the top: is it my fault that Amazon never furnished those photos to the person doing the pickup?

The update

Amazon's return-pickup app now shows the photos. An agent handling a wrong item sent return sees both the pictures the customer uploaded and images of the item that should have arrived — enough to judge at the door, independently, that the wrong thing was delivered.

Two emails, two features shipped. I'll take it.
