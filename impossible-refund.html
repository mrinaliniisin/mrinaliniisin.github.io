The impossible refund

"We couldn't complete your request this time. Please try again later." — the exact remedy the seller's own system offered, and then blocked.

Filed by Mrinalini Bengaluru · September 2026 ~6 min read

I bought two things from the label Old Marigold on Nykaa Fashion. One I loved. The other — a Meadow Cotton top in size M — didn't fit. What should have been a two-tap return turned into a twenty-day standoff between a marketplace, a brand, and a courier, each insisting the problem belonged to one of the others.

It ended with every rupee back. Here is exactly how it went, and the one move that turned it.

The trap

The listing's terms: no returns, replacement only — subject to availability. Reasonable enough on paper. Except when I went to replace the top, every size was sold out. So no replacement was possible — and, by their own logic, that meant no return and no refund either. The single remedy on offer was physically impossible, and every fallback door was locked.

The machine disagreed with its own staff

Here's the part that broke the case open. Nykaa's own return system did not believe the "no refund" line. When I hit Replace, it detected the item was out of stock, quietly routed me to a return, and offered ₹2,350 back to my original payment method.

The blame loop

Off-system, the humans ran a perfect circle. Nykaa's support said the vendor doesn't accept returns. The brand, on WhatsApp, said the order was placed on Nykaa, so take it up with them — and then told me to contact Myntra, a platform I had never used. Each party pointed at the other so that neither had to own the remedy.

So I filed — and things moved

I stopped arguing and filed a free grievance with the National Consumer Helpline against Nykaa Fashion, attaching the recording. The helpline isn't a court and can't order anyone to do anything — but the moment a government reference number was attached, the return I'd been denied for days appeared on its own.

Then the pickup "failed a quality check" it never reached

A reverse pickup was scheduled. The courier agent couldn't get past my building's gate and cancelled it, telling me it was now my problem. Minutes later, a message arrived: the return had failed the quality check, so the pickup was cancelled. The item had never left my house. It cannot fail an inspection it never reached — this was a false status logged to paper over a pickup that didn't happen.

I kept the top sealed, photographed it with tags intact, and put the contradiction in writing. Support then asked for a photo to open an "internal inquiry" — an admission the status was in doubt on their side too.

A promise in writing, then the money

Two support agents then committed, in writing, to a pickup within five business days and a refund within five to seven days of pickup. I replied holding them to their own dates, noted they'd never explained the false QC, and said my next message would contain the court filing. I set a hard deadline and left the real court option — a District Consumer Commission, free for small claims — loaded but unused.

What actually worked

Not winning the argument with whoever was in front of me. The pickup agent, the WhatsApp rep, the first-line support — none had the authority to fix anything, so every minute spent debating them was wasted. What moved it was building a record no script could survive.

  1. Stay polite, on the record. Every message factual and timestamped, so the pattern speaks for itself.
  2. Let them contradict themselves. The "no refund" policy versus their own refund page. "Keep it sealed for pickup" versus "failed quality check." Contradictions in their own words beat any complaint I could write.
  3. File early, escalate later. The helpline is free and produces an official paper trail; the grievance number alone made the return appear. The actual court option stayed loaded but unused.
  4. Make filing the obvious next step — then let them do the math. I never had to file the case. I only had to hold them to a written promise, with a deadline and a documented history of shifting excuses.

The takeaway

"No returns, replacement only" is not a policy when the item is out of stock. It's a way to keep your money. A remedy a company cannot deliver isn't a remedy — and being passed between a brand and a marketplace does not erase your right to a refund. You don't need to out-argue the person in front of you. You need a record, a reference number, and the patience to let the contradictions pile up.

Twenty days. One recorded screen. ₹2,350 back.
