Does a passenger aeroplane have a morgue?

No — commercial passenger flights don't have a dedicated morgue. If someone dies in flight, the body is typically moved to an empty seat or row when available and covered with a blanket; otherwise it stays in the person's own seat, handled as dignifiedly as space allows. One historical exception: some long-haul aircraft, such as the Singapore Airlines Airbus A340-500, had a special body compartment — sometimes nicknamed a “corpse cupboard” — fitted with straps and used only when no other suitable space was available. But that was a rare feature, not a standard morgue.

Won't the body start to decay before landing?

Not in any way you'd notice. Decay starts at the cellular level straight away, but the visible, smelly part, when gut bacteria fill the body with gas and it bloats, takes about two to four days at room temperature. Even the longest flights are under 20 hours.

Moving the body can push air left in the lungs past the vocal cords, making a sigh or groan. It's unsettling but means nothing.

Cabin pressure makes any gas already in the gut expand by about a quarter, the same thing that bloats living passengers. That isn't enough to cause a release.

If you end up sitting next to them

  • Call the crew first. If you aren't sure the person has died, press the call bell right away. If you know CPR, offer to help. Only a doctor can confirm a death, so crew may keep going until one does.
  • You can ask to move. Ask quietly and politely. On a full flight there may be nowhere to go, and the crew may move the body instead. Either way, asking is fine.
  • Don't touch the body or their things. Leave that to the crew. The body may be part of an investigation when you land.
  • Don't photograph or post. Somebody's family hasn't been told yet.
  • Twitches and sounds are normal. Air escaping and small muscle movements happen after death. If one really makes you doubt it, tell the crew rather than staying quiet.
  • Ask for what helps. A blanket over the body, a fresh blanket for yourself, water, or a few minutes in the galley. Crew would rather you ask.
  • Expect a short wait at landing. Passengers are often held on board while police or medical staff come on. You may be asked a few questions as a witness.
  • Afterwards, it's normal to feel shaken, even days later. Talk to someone about it. If the airline handled it badly, you can write to them; some offer an apology or compensation.
  • If you were travelling with them, the crew will let you stay close and will help you with the airline and the authorities on arrival.
