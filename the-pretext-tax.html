The Pretext Tax

Two men slid into my Instagram DMs this year. Neither said what he wanted. Both paid for it.

I'm not writing this to dunk on anyone. Names, faces and handles are blacked out. I'm writing it because I find it genuinely funny how often men get in their own way by refusing to state their intentions plainly. The hedge is supposed to protect them. It almost always does the opposite.

Exhibit A: "Did you get any warning?"

A stranger with a new account and a "Dr" in his name. We follow each other through one mutual friend. His opening line isn't hello, it's a question about Instagram's safety warnings, followed by the classic "you look very familiar."

I asked if he does this a lot. He says no, he just got a "restriction enabled" notice. Then he sends a view-once photo.

Then comes a compliment on my display picture and "where you from?" At that point I'd seen enough.

His response: he only messaged because of the warnings, to prove he isn't a robot. Lmao.

Exhibit B: The interview, then the swerve

The second man and I had met once, when a friend introduced us at their workplace. After that my posts started getting a lot of likes. Then he replied to a pole video of mine.

The chat is polite, even charming. How do you do this, how'd you get into it, is it one-on-one? Four or five interview questions. Then, out of nowhere: "Looking incredibly attractive by doing pole? 😛"

I didn't reply. Months later I ran into him at a restaurant, and he looked a bit worried.

Why the hedge backfires

The pretext is rejection insurance. If you ask a woman out and she says no, you got rejected. If you ask "did you get a warning?" and she ignores it, nothing happened. It was just a question. The hedge protects the ego, and it costs you your odds.

Everyone can see through it. Think of the guy at your door saying "I'm not selling anything, just a quick survey." You know it's a sales pitch, so the pretext doesn't lower your guard. It raises it. Now the question isn't only "do I want this?" but "why is he being sneaky?" Plain interest gets a fair hearing. Disguised interest reads as manipulation, even when it's harmless.

Deniability cuts both ways. "Looking incredibly attractive by doing pole?" can be a compliment (you look great doing pole) or a tease (you do pole to look attractive?). He gets to pick the meaning after he sees my reaction. That's the point of the hedge, and it's also why it lands so oddly. So does the pivot: neutral questions, then a sudden jump to attraction. It's like chatting with someone at a bus stop and then abruptly asking for their number.

It scales, and that shows. "You look familiar" and "loved your DP" can go to fifty women at almost no cost each. Stating intent means committing to one specific person.

Silence is the bill. A direct "want to grab a drink?" gets a clear yes or no, and you move on. An ambiguous line gets silence, and silence can't be decoded. Is she offended? Amused? Did she see it at all? Did she tell the friend who introduced us? That open tab is what the worried look at the restaurant was about, if that's what it was.

To be fair, a lot of men here have learned that asking directly is crude and that a pretext is the polite route. They probably think they're being decent.

Just say it

What both of them missed is that directness is attractive partly because it's rare. The man who writes "you seem interesting, want to grab coffee?" stands out because it costs him something. He might hear no. That risk is exactly what makes the ask feel sincere.

The hedge was meant to lower the risk. Instead it guaranteed the most awkward outcome: a woman who's suspicious, a question left hanging, and an uncomfortable run-in at a restaurant.

So, men of Instagram: ask plainly, take the answer, and move on. Your odds can only go up.
