Your Ex is not a second class citizen

August 12, 2026

Somewhere after it ends, the language shifts. They stop being a person and become a category — the narcissist, the avoidant one, the red flag you should have caught. Quietly, you've moved them down a tier: to a class of human whose feelings you no longer have to weigh and whose good qualities no longer count. Let's look at why we do it, and what it costs.

Why We Demote Them

The demotion isn't cruelty. It's anesthetic. You're holding two facts that won't sit together: I cared about this person and this person is gone, or hurt me, or I'm the one who left. The pain lives in the gap between them, so the mind reaches for the cheapest edit and makes the person smaller. If they were never worth much, the loss costs little.

Contempt, in other words, is a painkiller. The trouble is that it works — well enough that you forget it's a drug and start mistaking it for a diagnosis.

What "Second Class" Actually Means

Take the phrase literally. A second-class citizen is someone who lives in the same country as everyone else but has been stripped of standing — can't vote, can't be heard, has to use the side entrance. And that status was never a fact discovered about them. It was a decision made about them, by whoever held the power to decide, because it suited the people on top.

That is exactly what a breakup demotion is. You aren't discovering that your ex is a lesser person. You're passing a law that says so — and in this small country of your own head, you are the entire government. It felt like a finding. It was a decree.

What It Costs You

You have to falsify your own past to keep it. To believe they were always beneath you, you have to airbrush every hour that says otherwise — the good stretches, your own real reasons for choosing them. But those hours were yours too. Defacing the picture doesn't only lie about them; it lies about your life and your judgment, and you're the one left living in the smaller, falser memory.

Contempt is a leash, not distance. People treat scorn as the way out of someone. It's the opposite — you can't walk away from a person you're still busy prosecuting. Real distance is quieter than that. It looks like indifference, not a verdict.

Full-Sized and Still Gone

The reframe isn't "be friends" or "decide they were right." It's smaller and harder: let them stay a whole person, and let them still be gone. You can hold all of it at once — that they were real, that something in them was worth your time, that it didn't work, that one of you got hurt — and none of it lowers their rank in the species. A relationship can end without the other person losing their citizenship in the human race.

Let them be a whole person. Just not your person anymore. It costs you nothing, and it hands back your own history, told straight.
