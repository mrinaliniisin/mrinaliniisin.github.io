A Relaxed Woman Knows When Her Clothes Are "Dirty"

September 1, 2026

There's a shirt on my chair right now that I wore outside yesterday. It has company — a whole staging area of clothes I've worn once, sitting there until they've been worn enough to actually earn the laundry basket. A middle category. A year ago that middle category didn't exist: worn outside meant straight to the basket, and I'd have thought nothing of it, because I thought that was just me. Tidy. Particular. A person with standards.

It wasn't me. It was fear wearing my clothes.

The rule I never questioned

The rule was simple and I never wrote it down because rules you never question don't need writing down: worn outside means dirty means wash. One wear, then the basket. It felt like hygiene. It felt like being a competent adult.

What it actually was: a small machine for converting anxiety into laundry.

Something out there touched the shirt, so the shirt was flagged, so the flag had to be discharged, and washing discharged it. Clean shirt, quiet nervous system. The catch is that the machine never turns off. Wear, flag, wash, repeat — and the repeating is the whole cost. I was permanently mid-cycle: laundry into cooking into cleaning into laundry, a treadmill I mistook for a personality. The extended, unbroken blocks of time I wanted for my own work never showed up, because the machine kept spending them a load at a time.

Where the machine came from

I know exactly where I got it. As a kid I'd ask my mother — out loud — whether I was allowed to hug her, because she'd bathed that day: bathed meant clean meant do-not-contaminate. I absorbed that untouchability the way children absorb everything, not as a belief but as a reflex, and my own anxiety fed it over the years until it had generalized into the whole outside world is post-bath. But the contamination frame was the smaller wound. The larger one is this: a child turned away when she reaches for her mother is being starved of something she needs in order to grow, and that need doesn't expire when she stops crawling. Held at arm's length, a kid learns she is too much, or not enough, to be touched. I carried the deficit into adulthood and went looking to fill it in boyfriends — quietly baffled when they wouldn't hold my hand, and when one finally did, I stuck to him even as he pushed me away with almost exactly my mother's aggression. It felt familiar, and I mistook familiar for home.

And a world that keeps proving it right

Here's what kept the rule watered: the outside world genuinely is an assault. This is Bangalore. Going out means driving braced — someone coming the wrong way, someone running the red, someone stopped dead across your turn, all of it done nonchalantly, as if none of it matters — so you carry the vigilance for everyone. Add honking used as a second language, streets ringed with filth and littered garbage. A full-body sensory battering, and more so when you already carry anxiety into it. You come home wrung out. And the shirt comes home carrying a physical trace of the ordeal: a mildly damp underarm, the somatic residue of two hours of bracing.

So the damp patch isn't nothing. It's a sensor reading. There is a signal there. The problem was never that I was imagining dirt — it's that the sensor was calibrated too hot. A nervous system already braced against "outside contaminates" reads mild dampness after a hard outing as dirty, wash now.

Think of a smoke detector wired too sensitive. It isn't lying when it shrieks at toast — there really is smoke. It's just calling a non-fire a fire. My mother built the detector. Bangalore supplies the toast, daily.

What the medication actually changed

Here's the part that made me want to write this down. When the amitriptyline pulled the anxiety down, I didn't decide to start re-wearing clothes. I didn't resolve to be more relaxed about it. I just... noticed, weeks in, that the shirt on the chair had stopped bothering me. The flag wasn't firing. So I wore it again.

Think of a sprained ankle. You limp — not because limping is how you walk, but because the ankle hurts and the limp protects it. To anyone watching, the limp is your gait. Heal the ankle and the limp vanishes on its own; you don't have to relearn walking, and no one says your personality changed.

The laundry rule was a limp. The anxiety was the sprain. Lower the pain and the protective behavior just… stops, because there's nothing left to protect against.

The medication didn't unplug the detector. It reset the sensitivity. Same damp underarm, same input — but now it registers as worn, fine to wear again instead of contaminated, wash now. The reading didn't change. The threshold did.

Which tells me something the tidy-person story never could: it was never a trait. Traits don't dissolve in a few weeks because of a small orange pill. Symptoms do.

What "relaxed" turned out to mean

I used to think relaxed meant calm — a mood, a softness. Turns out, for me, it means something more structural: fewer machines running in the background, converting my time and attention into tasks I never consciously chose.

The relaxed woman isn't lazier about her clothes. She's not ignoring dirt, either. She can just tell the difference now — between a shirt that's actually dirty and a shirt that only felt dirty because the world outside was loud and the sensor was set too hot. Real dirty still goes in the basket. It always did. What left is the false-positive: the damp-underarm verdict I used to trust without ever checking it.

Because the detector still goes off — and when it does now, there's really a fire. My pole class leotard comes home soaked through; that goes straight in, no hesitation, because it is dirty. Underwear I change twice a day, non-negotiable, always have. And the once-worn shirt goes back on the chair. Same sensor, three different readings, each one correct. That's the tell that it's working — not that it stays quiet, but that it fires when it should and only when it should.

That's the whole shift, and it's smaller and stranger than "calm." I know when my clothes are dirty. For thirty years I thought I already did.

The shirt's still on the chair. It's fine. I'm going to wear it today — and the block of time I'd have spent washing it, I'm spending here, writing this.
