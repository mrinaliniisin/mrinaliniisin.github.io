Let's Eat!

August 21, 2026

Growing up I was reed thin, thanks to several bouts of malaria before I even turned 10. My hands and feet were always cold, a body that worried the adults around me. Yenu thinnode-illa, my mother would say. She doesn't eat anything. When aunts pressed food on me I'd negotiate them down to uppu thuppa anna — salt, ghee, rice — and I wouldn't touch even that unless someone told me a story while I ate.

I only understood the mechanism decades later. Food would go in when something else was gently holding my attention — a story in my ear, my mind occupied elsewhere. The eating happened in the shelter of another channel. That has been my operating system since before I can properly remember, and everything that came later was a variation on it.

The first time food left

I lost someone close to me in May 2016. I also lost my familiar friends' circle and I started living alone. My mother didn't understand what I was mourning. I didn't yet know that therapy was a thing, that a psychiatrist might help, that my own mind was something a person could get support for. So I simply suffered it, by myself, and my appetite went with it.

What brought me back that first time wasn't a tool — I had none. It was my bff, who came into my life that same year and quietly folded me into her circle, plus the slow work of time. That is worth marking, because she is the thread that runs through this whole story. The first person to bring me back to the table, years before any of the rest of it.

When food started losing to talking

The next time, it wasn't grief. It was a relationship that had ended, and the anxiety of being near the person afterwards.

Eating felt like theft. Every bite was time I wasn't spending saying the thing I needed to say. Talking felt scarce, urgent, running out — as if I'd never get enough of it to empty myself of everything I had to tell him. A forkful of food was a forkful of stolen minutes. My mouth had one job, and talking always won.

It wasn't only him. With new people I'd talk too much, watch myself wearing them out, and be unable to stop — especially once some memory surfaced and my mind wanted to tell all of it at once. Food just sat there being an impediment. The same mouth, the same scarcity, the same outcome: talking won, eating waited.

Two engines

The appetite came back from two directions at once. In early 2024 my psychiatrist worked out that the medication I was on wasn't holding me — I was still reacting hard to every rude thing the person I'd been with did — and moved me onto a different one. On the new medication I found I could hold my fort when he tried to wound me; and once the brain chemistry had settled under me, I understood the rest was mine to build. It was time to train the behaviour and the feelings themselves — around rejection, around loss, around grief. Chemistry could steady me. It couldn't do that part.

The same month I changed medication, I began pole.

Pole matters more than it first looks. As a girl I was kept out of dance: my mother, anxious about her own body, feared that if I took up Bharatanatyam and later quit, I'd get fat — so she pre-emptively said no. The one channel she closed on me was the joyful use of my own body. So there's a quiet symmetry in the fact that one of the two engines that gave me my appetite back as an adult was movement — the exact thing that had been managed out of my childhood. Pole wasn't only training. It was taking back a no.

The medication switched hunger on chemically. Pole did it physically — real muscular work emptying the tank, the honest kind of hunger with no anxiety anywhere near it. So part of the appetite was done to me, and part of it I built. Through 2025 I gained weight steadily and settled at a comfortable 61kg: full portions I couldn't have finished before, muscle on my frame, meals I actually enjoyed.

I didn't know it at the time, but all of that year was preparation. 2025 turned out to be a dumpster fire, and at the centre of it was my father — his hospitalisation, then his death that spring. The hospital was enormous, a labyrinth of buildings and maze-like floors. When he was admitted I was awake for thirty-six hours straight, and at one point in the dead of night I was sent to fetch his medications, walking that whole maze alone. My legs were giving out under me. I chanted Thandey shakti kodu — Father, give me strength — and I knew, even in that moment, that the strength wasn't only coming from some higher power. It was coming from within me.

That is what the movement and the food had quietly been laying down in me all the year before. The strength wasn't vanity. It was reserve — what I ran on when there was nothing left.

None of it, though, taught me to eat in a room full of strangers. That part I still had to learn.

The hard rooms

The real skill isn't eating peacefully next to people who love you. It's doing it in a room that scares you.

Early 2025, a new job abroad, dining with strangers — around the time I was working out how genuinely bad the job was. I remember telling myself: ignore them all, and just enjoy the food. That isn't "eat instead of over-talk." That's using food to withdraw my investment from a room that hadn't earned it.

And the same city that gave me that cold table also gave me a friend who took me, outing after outing, to its hidden gems. So it was two rooms, not one: the table I ate to escape, and the tables I ate to lean into. The same skill pointed in opposite directions — and both let me eat.

Now I catch it happening. The room used to own my mouth: talk, urgently, or starve. Now I decide what a room gets — my conversation, or just my quiet company — and either way, I get fed. That's not appetite returning. That's the locus of control moving inside.

Dressing for myself

The same shift was happening in what I wore, on the same timeline.

I wore a saree for someone else for the first time around 2021 — for the person I was with. For his eyes. Then the relationship ended, my bff moved out for a while, and in the clearing after both departures I started draping sarees on my own. The month I began pole was the month I first wore a saree entirely for myself. One using my body, one adorning it — both mine.

When the weight came in 2025, the sarees fit properly, and I looked, finally, elegant — like a woman. And that is when I started feeling like one. Notice where it came from: not from being looked at. Every earlier version had been for someone else's gaze. The version that landed arrived when I had flesh on my frame, movement in my body, and no one to dress for. I felt like a woman at the point my body became mine rather than watched.

I even started correcting myself when my mind said girl. Not to kill the playful child in me — I can still drop into her with someone I trust — but to place her. I'm a woman now; I know when to bring my femininity to a moment and when to let the child come out to play. I hold the switch. Same as eating in any mode now, same as pole: the capacity to choose, dependence on none.

What the world started reflecting back

Once I could eat in hard rooms, the world started handing the ease back to me. Travelling, a stranger's mother wanted a photo of me with her small daughter — she liked how easily I spoke. I didn't audition for it; I just received it. And people who know me started saying how well I looked since I'd gained weight. The precise thing anxiety used to starve — my body, my appetite — is now what people read as wellness. The starvation had left a visible absence; the eating left a visible presence, and they clocked it correctly.

From cooking to being cooked for

Before any of this, I did have a relationship with food — but it ran one direction. From 2017 onward I baked, cooked, fed people, carried food into the office, leaning hard on being liked. For six years, food was the currency I paid to earn a place. Later I cooked the same way for the person I was with and for my bff — out of love, but also out of a responsibility I'd put on myself.

It was never for me. You can't be nourished by an act of service you're performing — the satisfaction never felt like enough, because I was always on the giving end, never the receiving one.

These days it's turning around. My bff is learning to be the cook for both of us, and I step in only when she needs a hand. I let myself be fed. For someone who habitually takes the provider seat, that is harder than eating ever was — putting down the tray and letting somebody look after me.

The signal, and the second override

I'd love to end there, cured. But the real lesson is subtler. The whole problem, from the start, was overriding my body's signal. Grief and anxiety taught me to override the no — I'm hungry but I won't eat. Once that was fixed, a new override showed up in a different costume: ignoring the yes.

Back home, jaded — worn thin abroad, then my father's death — cooking just for myself felt like too much. So I ignored the hunger pangs, and the body escalated from a whisper to a shout: a few severe bouts of gastritis. It stopped the day I answered it. Homemade South Indian meals — rice, dals, poriyals. Sipping water through the day. The bloating settled almost immediately, as if the body had just been waiting to be listened to.

So this isn't a story about anxiety being cured. It's the ongoing, unglamorous work of hearing the signal at all — and noticing that the thing blocking me from hearing it keeps changing outfits. First grief, then anxiety, then fatigue. The muscle is the same either way: listen, then feed yourself.

For years I told myself airplane bread was too dry — tasted it once a decade ago, filed the verdict, stopped checking. But my appetite had come up since then, and hunger is its own seasoning: food simply tastes better now. A little butter turns even a plain roll edible — not the sourdough I actually love, but good enough to want. So these days I butter the roll and find out for myself. Stop repeating the old verdict, check what's in front of you, feed yourself well.

I'm still training it. But these days, mostly, I eat.

And when I travel now, I keep a small ritual that belongs to no one but me. Everywhere I land, I try the local ice cream — and I walk, by myself, to go get a cone. Not to be polite. Not to keep a seat or fill a silence. Just because I want it. Those solo walks have left me a whole trail of memories, one scoop at a time, city after city, each cone held in my own hand in the sun. The satisfaction that never felt like enough back when I was only ever feeding other people — it turns out it was always waiting on the far side of a single word. Mine.

[^1]: The title borrows from Let's Eat, the Korean drama of the same name — a show I loved through the COVID years, all three seasons of it.
